# Practice Kit · Five-Minute Setup

> You cannot learn knots by reading about them, any more than you can learn to swim from a diagram. The documents in this course carry the *understanding*; your hands carry the *skill*. This page is the shortest path to having something to tie.

## The minimum kit

**Two pieces of cord, different colors, ~2 m (6 ft) each, 5–7 mm diameter.**

Why these numbers:

- **Two, in different colors.** Half the knots in this course join two ropes or pass one rope around another. If both are the same color, you cannot see what you did — and worse, you cannot see what you did *wrong*. Color-coding your two strands is the single highest-leverage thing on this page. Red and blue, or black and yellow.
- **~2 m.** Long enough to tie anything in this course with slack to spare; short enough not to become a tangle on the couch.
- **5–7 mm.** Below about 4 mm your fingers are working at the edge of their resolution and everything is fiddly. Above about 10 mm the cord is stiff and hard to dress. 6 mm is the sweet spot for learning hands.
- **Braided, not laid (twisted).** Laid three-strand rope is beautiful and traditional, but it kinks, and it hides the structure of a knot inside its own twist. Braided cord shows you the geometry.

Good cheap options: 6 mm accessory cord from a climbing shop (best feel, holds a shape, most durable), 6 mm braided nylon utility cord from a hardware store, or paracord (550 cord — very available, slightly slick and squishy, acceptable).

**Seal the ends.** Cut ends unravel and then the cord is trash. Melt synthetic ends briefly with a lighter and roll them (careful — molten nylon burns), or wrap each end tightly with a turn of electrical tape.

## Worth adding later

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| A third, thicker cord (~10–12 mm, 1 m) | Bends behave differently between *unequal* diameters — that's the whole reason the sheet bend exists |
| A metal ring, a large carabiner, or a bucket handle | Hitches need something to hitch to; a ring also demonstrates ring-loading failure |
| A chair leg / stair rail / broom handle | A round spar for hitches. A chair back is fine. |
| A short length of slippery cord (Dyneema/HMPE, or slick polyester) | Later in the course we test which knots fail on modern low-friction cordage. Optional but instructive. |
| Something with real weight — a full backpack, a water jug | Knots that feel fine at hand tension behave differently at 20 kg. Loading a knot and then trying to untie it teaches jamming faster than any paragraph can. |

## Reference material for the hands

The documents in this course deliberately don't try to replace a moving image — text is a bad medium for "pass the end *under* and *back through*." Pair the reading with one of these:

- **Animated Knots by Grog** (web / app) — step-by-step photo animations, the de facto standard reference. Use it for the *motion*; use these lessons for the *why*.
- **Clifford Ashley, *The Ashley Book of Knots* (1944)** — 3,800+ knots, still the definitive reference work seventy years on. Overkill as a learning tool, unmatched as a place to look things up and to feel the depth of the subject.
- **Geoffrey Budworth, *The Complete Book of Knots*** — the sane, practical middle: a few hundred knots with clear drawings and honest notes on when each one is a bad idea.

## One rule before you start

Whenever you tie a knot in this course, tie it, then **load it hard**, then **try to untie it**. Three seconds of pulling teaches you more about a knot's real character — does it slip, does it capsize, does it jam solid — than any amount of looking at it. Most of what people "know" about knots is knowledge of how they look when new and unloaded, which is precisely the state that never matters.
