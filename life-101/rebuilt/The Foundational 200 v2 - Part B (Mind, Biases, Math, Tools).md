The Foundational 200 v2 - Part B (Mind, Biases, Math, Tools)
    IV The Human Mind (Psychology)
        Neuroplasticity :: The brain's ability to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections throughout life.
            Neuroplasticity — is the brain fixed or malleable? >> Malleable. It rewires in response to experience and learning throughout life, not only in childhood.
            Neuroplasticity — why it matters for learning? >> Abilities are built, not merely revealed: repeated practice physically strengthens the circuits involved. The same wiring process is why long-running habits, good and bad, get harder to change.
        Growth Mindset :: The belief that abilities can be developed through effort and practice.
            Growth Mindset — what does it contrast with? >> A Fixed Mindset.
            Growth Mindset — what behaviors does it encourage? >> Embracing challenges, persisting through setbacks, and treating failure as information for learning.
            Growth vs. Fixed Mindset — who popularized the framework? >> Psychologist Carol Dweck.
            Growth-mindset interventions — do they actually raise achievement? >> Contested: large meta-analyses (Sisk et al. ##theory
                2018) found weak average effects, and the 2019 National Study of Learning Mindsets found real but modest gains concentrated in lower-achieving students. A useful attitude, not a panacea.
        Fixed Mindset :: The belief that abilities are innate and unchangeable.
        Human Memory :: Reconstructive, not a perfect recording — the brain rebuilds each memory at the moment of recall.
            Human memory — what happens to a memory each time it is recalled? >> It is reassembled and can change slightly. Recalling a memory is partly rewriting it.
            Human memory — practical implication? >> For anything important, trust records over recollections: write it down. Two sincere people can honestly "remember" the same event differently.
        Active Recall (in learning) :: Retrieving information from memory — self-testing, flashcards, closed-book explaining — rather than passively re-reading it.
            Active Recall — how effective is it as a study technique? >> Among the best-replicated, most effective study techniques known: the act of retrieval itself strengthens the memory (the "testing effect").
        Spaced Repetition (in learning) :: Reviewing information at increasing intervals over time to improve long-term retention.
            Which two study techniques does learning science rate most effective? >> 1) Practice testing (active recall) 2) Distributed practice (spacing). Both rated "high utility" in Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 review, while the most popular techniques — re-reading, highlighting — rated low.
        Habit Loop? >>>
            - Cue (trigger)
            - Routine (behavior)
            - Reward
            Habit Loop — the Cue? >> The trigger that starts the routine: a time, a place, an emotional state, or a preceding action.
            Habit Loop — the Reward? >> The payoff that teaches the brain this loop is worth remembering and repeating.
            Changing a habit — the standard method? >> Keep the Cue and the Reward, but change the Routine between them.
        Compound Interest and Habit — how are they the same mechanism? >> Each cycle's small gain is added to the base, so the next cycle starts from a slightly larger base: money compounds in an account, skill and character compound in a person.
            Same exponential curve at different scales — the daily rep is the deposit.
        Delayed Gratification :: The ability to resist an immediate reward in favor of a larger reward later.
            The marshmallow test :: Walter Mischel's Stanford experiments (1960s-70s): preschoolers chose one treat now, or two if they could wait about 15 minutes alone with the first one.
            Marshmallow test — does early delay ability predict later life success? >> Contested: the famous correlation shrank in the 2018 Watts/Duncan/Quan replication (larger, more diverse sample) ##theory
                already half the original size before controls, then reduced by about two-thirds once family background, home environment, and early cognitive ability were controlled. Much of "willpower predicts success" was family circumstances predicting both.
        Hedonic Adaptation :: The tendency of humans to return quickly to a stable baseline level of happiness despite major positive or negative life events.
            Hedonic Adaptation — another name? >> The Hedonic Treadmill.
            Hedonic adaptation — practical implication? >> Purchases and wins buy less lasting happiness than predicted — the baseline resets and the chase resumes. It also works in your favor: setbacks sting for less time than feared.
        Cognitive Dissonance :: The mental discomfort experienced when holding contradictory beliefs, or when actions contradict beliefs.
            Cognitive dissonance — how do people typically resolve it? >> By changing their beliefs to align with their actions, or by rationalizing the behavior.
        A foundational human need (psychology) :: Theory: the need to belong and connect — strong social ties are among the best-supported predictors of health and longevity, though frameworks differ on whether belonging is the single deepest need. ##theory
            Strong social ties — what are they vital for? >> Health and longevity.
        Emotional Intelligence (EQ) :: The ability to understand and manage your own emotions, and to recognize and influence the emotions of others.
        Flow State :: A state of complete immersion in an activity, characterized by energized focus and enjoyment.
            Flow state — when does it occur? >> When challenge and skill are balanced: the task is hard enough to demand full attention, not so hard it overwhelms.
            Flow state — what happens when challenge and skill are mismatched? >> Challenge far above skill produces anxiety; challenge far below skill produces boredom. Flow lives in the channel between.
        Locus of Control :: The degree to which people believe they control their own destiny versus being controlled by external forces.
            Internal locus of control :: The belief that outcomes mostly depend on your own actions and choices.
            External locus of control :: The belief that outcomes are mostly determined by outside forces — luck, other people, the system.
    V Cognitive Biases and Flaws
        Cognitive bias :: A systematic, predictable error pattern in human thinking — not a random mistake but a consistent tilt built into the brain's fast shortcuts.
        Cognitive biases — why learn them by name? >> Your mind runs fast, not accurately, and fails in known, nameable ways. A named failure mode can be caught live, mid-commission; an unnamed one just feels like thinking.
        Confirmation Bias :: The tendency to search for, interpret, and recall information in a way that confirms one's pre-existing beliefs.
        Sunk Cost Fallacy :: Continuing an endeavor because of previously invested resources (time, money, effort), even when the current costs outweigh the future benefits.
        Availability Heuristic :: A mental shortcut where we overestimate the likelihood of events that are easily recalled — recent, vivid, or memorable ones.
        Dunning-Kruger Effect :: A bias where people with low ability at a task overestimate their competence, while experts may underestimate theirs.
        Anchoring Bias :: The tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information offered (the "anchor") when making decisions.
        Fundamental Attribution Error :: The tendency to attribute others' behavior to their character, while attributing your own behavior to the situation.
        Self-Serving Bias :: Attributing your successes to your own skill, but your failures to external factors.
        Halo Effect :: When a positive impression of a person in one area influences your opinion of them in unrelated areas.
        Survivorship Bias :: Focusing only on the successes of a selection process and ignoring the failures, producing a skewed picture of reality.
        Narrative Fallacy :: The human tendency to create simple stories to explain complex realities, often seeing patterns where none exist.
        Base Rate Fallacy :: Ignoring general statistical information (the base rate) and focusing instead on the specific, vivid individual case when making a judgment.
        Spotlight Effect :: The tendency to believe you are being noticed more than you really are.
        Straw Man Argument :: A logical fallacy where one misrepresents an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.
        Ad Hominem Attack :: A logical fallacy where one attacks the character of the person making the argument rather than the argument itself.
        Appeal to Authority Fallacy :: Insisting a claim is true simply because an authority said it, without other supporting evidence — or when the authority is not an expert on the topic.
    VII Mathematics and Probability
        Exponential Growth :: Growth whose rate is proportional to the current size — the bigger it gets, the faster it grows.
            Exponential growth — contrasted with what? >> Linear growth. Linear adds a constant amount per period; exponential multiplies by a constant percentage per period.
            Why is understanding exponential growth crucial? >> It runs compound interest, population dynamics, pandemics, and technological advancement — and human intuition is linear, so we chronically underestimate it.
                (The Rule of 72 — see Life Skills: Personal Finance — turns any growth rate into a doubling time.)
        Conditional Probability :: The probability of one thing given that another is already known to have happened — written P(A given B). New information shrinks the space of possibilities.
            Why is P(A given B) not the same as P(B given A)? >> They ask different questions of different groups.
                Nearly all NBA players are tall; almost no tall people are NBA players. Swapping the two conditionals is the error underneath the Base Rate Fallacy and the prosecutor's fallacy.
            Prosecutor's Fallacy? >> Presenting the probability of the evidence assuming innocence ("only 1 in a million innocent people would match") as if it were the probability of innocence given the evidence
                an inverted conditional that ignores how many people are in the pool.
            Independent events :: Events where knowing one occurred tells you nothing about the other — P(A given B) equals plain P(A). Coin flips are independent.
        Base Rate :: The underlying frequency of an event or trait in the general population, before you look at the specific case in front of you.
            Base Rate (as a thinking tool) — patches which failure mode? >> The Availability Heuristic, and its cousin the Base Rate Fallacy: vivid, recent cases hijack your probability estimates.
                The patch is asking "how often does this actually happen in general?" before weighing the story in front of you.
        Bayesian Updating :: The rule for revising beliefs in the light of evidence: the posterior equals the prior multiplied by the strength of the evidence (then normalized).
            Beliefs are degrees of confidence that move with evidence, not true/false switches.
            Prior probability (Bayes) :: Your probability estimate before the new evidence arrives. For a diagnosis, the base rate is the natural prior.
            Posterior probability (Bayes) :: Your updated probability after weighing the new evidence. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior.
            Strength of evidence (Bayes) :: How much more likely the observation is if the hypothesis is true than if it is false. Evidence equally likely either way moves nothing, however dramatic it feels.
            Bayes medical-test example — the setup and the surprise? >> A disease affects 1% of people; the test catches 90% of true cases and false-alarms on 9% of healthy people.
                You test positive. Intuition says you are probably sick; the true chance is only about 9%.
            Bayes medical-test example — the natural-frequency walk-through? >> Out of 1,000 people: 10 are sick, and 9 of them test positive; of the 990 healthy, about 89 also test positive.
                So 9 true cases out of roughly 98 positives — about 9%.
            Bayes medical-test example — why is the posterior so low? >> Because the posterior is anchored to the prior (the 1% base rate).
                When a condition is rare, false alarms from the huge healthy majority outnumber true hits from the tiny sick minority — a strong test cannot rescue a tiny prior by itself.
            Bayes medical-test example — what does a second independent positive do? >> The roughly 9% posterior becomes the new prior, and a second positive lifts it to roughly 50%.
                Updating is iterative: accumulating independent evidence is how a belief legitimately climbs from unlikely toward near-certain.
            Natural frequencies — the trick for base-rate problems? >> Convert percentages into counts of people ("out of 1,000 people..."). Problems that defeat intuition as probabilities become easy as counts.
            Bayesian updating — why is it the formal engine of intellectual humility? >> It makes "I might be wrong" operational: every belief carries a probability, every piece of evidence moves it, and certainty is never reached — only better-calibrated confidence.
        Expected Value :: The probability-weighted average of all possible outcomes of a decision.
            Expected value — how is it calculated? >> Multiply each potential outcome by the probability of it occurring, then sum the results.
            Expected value — how do asymmetric bets fool the win-rate instinct? >> A bet can lose most of the time and still be excellent (small stake, huge payoff), or win most of the time and still be terrible (small gain, ruinous tail).
                Price both branches, not the win rate.
            Expected value — when is it the wrong guide? >> When one branch is ruin.
                A positive-expected-value gamble that can wipe you out is still a bad bet: you live one path, not the average of all paths, and ruin ends the game — no future bets to average over.
        Law of Large Numbers :: As the number of trials increases, the average of the results obtained approaches the expected value.
            Law of Large Numbers — what real-world business does it explain? >> Why casinos always make money in the long run: each game carries a small house edge, and enough trials force the average toward that edge.
                Individual gamblers face variance; the house faces arithmetic.
            Law of Large Numbers — what does it NOT say? >> That short runs even out, or that a streak is "due" to reverse (the gambler's fallacy).
                Chance has no memory: convergence happens by dilution across many future trials, not by correction.
        Sample size — the first question to ask any impressive statistic? >> "Based on how many?" Small samples are noisy, so the most extreme results in any ranking
            best small schools, hottest new funds — tend to come from the smallest groups, by chance alone.
        Selection Effect :: When the sample you see was filtered before it reached you, so it is not a random draw.
            The sample then tells you as much about the filter as about reality.
            Selection effects — the WWII bomber illustration ;; Returning bombers showed bullet holes on wings and fuselage, so commanders wanted armor there.
                Statistician Abraham Wald's insight: armor where the survivors show few holes — planes hit in those spots never made it home. The data was filtered by survival.
            Selection effects — where do you meet them daily? >> Testimonials (only the satisfied speak), success books (only survivors write memoirs), online reviews (the furious and the delighted), "everyone I know agrees" (you chose who you know).
                Ask what was filtered out before the sample reached you.
        Normal Distribution :: A common probability distribution where most data points cluster around the average, and the further from the average, the fewer the data points.
            Normal distribution — another name? >> The Bell Curve.
            Normal distribution — the share of values within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean? >> About 68% within 1 SD, about 95% within 2, about 99.7% within 3 (the empirical rule).
        Standard Deviation :: A measure of the amount of variation or dispersion in a set of values.
            A low standard deviation indicates what? >> Data points are close to the average.
            A high standard deviation indicates what? >> Data points are spread out over a wide range of values.
        Regression to the Mean :: If a variable is extreme on its first measurement, it will tend to be closer to the average on the second — because chance contributed to the extreme.
            Regression to the mean — the trap it sets? >> After any extreme result, whatever you did next looks causal, because the next result was drifting back toward average anyway: the remedy taken at the fever's peak, the scolding that "fixed" a bad game, the magazine-cover jinx.
        Diminishing Returns :: The point where each additional unit of input yields less additional benefit than the unit before — the marginal return is declining.
            Diminishing returns — does it mean the effort has stopped paying? >> No. Declining marginal returns means each extra unit helps less than the last
                the total can still be well worth the total cost. "Not worth doing at all" (total benefit below total cost) is a different, stronger claim — diminishing returns only tells you when to stop adding more.
    VI The Tools of Thought (Logic, Models, Strategy)
        Tools of thought — why do you need them if you already know your biases? >> Knowing a bias does not switch it off — the failure happens faster than self-observation.
            The tools are external procedures (ask what would prove you wrong, check the base rate, steel-man first) that work even when you cannot feel yourself failing.
        The five patch-pairs — which tool patches which failure mode? >>>
            - Falsifiability patches Confirmation Bias
            - Base Rate (section VII) patches the Availability Heuristic
            - Hanlon's Razor patches the Fundamental Attribution Error
            - Steel-manning patches the Straw Man
            - Second-Order Thinking patches the Narrative Fallacy
        Scientific Method? >>>
            - Observation
            - Hypothesis
            - Experimentation
            - Analysis
            - Conclusion and revision
            Scientific method — why does the last step say "revision," not just "conclusion"? >> The method never certifies final truth; it keeps the best-surviving explanation on probation. Every conclusion remains open to overthrow by new evidence — that is the feature, not a weakness.
        Falsifiability :: For a hypothesis to be scientific, it must be inherently disprovable — some possible observation must be able to prove it false.
            Falsifiability — which philosopher made it the test of science? >> Karl Popper: if no conceivable evidence could prove a claim false, the claim is not scientific.
            Falsifiability — patches which failure mode? >> Confirmation Bias. The bias hunts for evidence that agrees with you; falsifiability forces the opposite question — "what would prove me wrong?" — and sends you looking for that instead.
        First Principles Thinking :: Breaking a complex problem down into its most basic, foundational elements and reasoning up from there, rather than reasoning by analogy ("how it's usually done").
        Occam's Razor :: When competing explanations account for the evidence equally well, prefer the one that requires the fewest assumptions. Other things being equal, do not multiply entities beyond necessity.
            Occam's Razor — what does it NOT say? >> Not "the simplest explanation is always true." It is a tie-breaker among explanations that fit the evidence equally well
                an explanation that fits the facts worse does not win by being simpler.
        Correlation :: When two things tend to occur together.
            What is the answer on correlation imply causation? >> No — The association may be coincidence, or both variables may be driven by a third factor.
            Confounder (third variable) :: A hidden factor that drives both correlated variables. Ice cream sales and drownings rise together because summer drives both — neither causes the other.
        Causation :: When one thing actually produces the other — established by intervention and experiment, not by association alone.
        Second-Order Thinking :: Thinking beyond the immediate consequences of an action, to the effects of those consequences.
            Second-order thinking — the question it asks? >> "And then what?"
            Second-order thinking — patches which failure mode? >> The Narrative Fallacy. A tidy story stops at the first consequence
                asking "and then what?" forces out the longer chains the story leaves out — which is where the real outcome usually lives.
        Pareto Principle :: Roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.
            Pareto Principle — another name? >> The 80/20 Rule.
            Pareto Principle — how to apply it? >> Identify and focus on the high-leverage 20% of activities that produce 80% of the results.
        Systems Thinking :: A holistic approach that focuses on how a system's parts interrelate and how the system behaves over time, rather than just looking at isolated components.
        Feedback Loop :: When the outputs of a system are routed back in as inputs, shaping the next round of behavior.
            Positive Feedback Loop :: A feedback loop that amplifies change in the system.
                Positive feedback loop — examples ;; Compound interest; panic buying. Each round of change fuels a bigger next round.
            Negative Feedback Loop :: A feedback loop that stabilizes the system, resisting change.
                Negative feedback loop — examples ;; A thermostat; body temperature regulation (homeostasis). Deviation triggers a correction back toward the set point.
        Hanlon's Razor :: Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by carelessness, incompetence, or misunderstanding.
            Hanlon's Razor — patches which failure mode? >> The Fundamental Attribution Error — the reflex that reads others' behavior as character ("they did that because they're hostile") instead of situation.
                The razor makes you check the boring situational explanation before the sinister one.
        "The Map is Not the Territory"? >> The description of a thing — any model or abstraction — is not the thing itself.
            Map and territory — are models of reality ever perfect? >> No. Models are useful reductions, never perfect; the danger begins when you forget which one you are holding.
        Via Negativa :: The principle that we often gain more wisdom, or improve a system more, by subtracting — removing errors, bad habits, and exposure — rather than by adding.
        Antifragility :: A property of systems that increase in capability or robustness as a result of stressors, shocks, or volatility.
            Antifragile systems benefit from what? >> Disorder (Nassim Taleb). Beyond resilience: the resilient survives shocks unchanged, while the antifragile gets stronger because of them — muscles, immune systems, tested ideas.
        Skin in the Game :: Having a measurable personal risk when making a decision.
            Skin in the game — what does it ensure? >> Accountability, and alignment of incentives. Discount advice from anyone who pays no price for being wrong.
        Steel-Manning (Principle of Charity) :: In argumentation, interpreting a speaker's statements in the most rational and strongest way possible before criticizing them.
            Steel-manning — why it strengthens your own position? >> If your rebuttal defeats the strongest version of the other side's case, the rebuttal actually binds; defeating a weak version proves nothing.
            Steel-Manning — patches which failure mode? >> The Straw Man Argument. Rebuilding the strongest version of the other side's case makes the caricature impossible — and makes your rebuttal, if it still stands, actually binding.
        Tragedy of the Commons :: A situation where individuals, acting in self-interest, deplete a shared resource contrary to the common good.
            Tragedy of the commons — examples? >> Overfishing; pollution.
            Tragedy of the commons — the standard escapes? >>>
                - Ownership (property rights internalize the cost)
                - Regulation (quotas, caps)
                - Community norms with monitoring (Elinor Ostrom's documented commons). All three change the incentives; none relies on asking people to be nicer
        Prisoner's Dilemma (Game Theory) :: A game-theory scenario showing why two rational individuals might not cooperate, even when cooperation appears to be in both parties' best interest.
            Prisoner's Dilemma — why does defection win a single round? >> Whatever the other player does, each player is individually better off defecting — so two rational players both defect and land on an outcome worse for both than mutual cooperation.
            Prisoner's Dilemma — what changes when the game repeats? >> Repetition lets cooperation be enforced: today's defection can be punished tomorrow (tit-for-tat), so the shadow of the future makes cooperating rational. One-shot interactions are where behavior gets ugly.
        Natural Selection and economic incentives — what single algorithm do both run? >> Differential survival under selection pressure.
            Whatever the environment rewards, replicates: organisms that capture resources leave more offspring; behaviors and firms that capture payoffs get repeated and copied. To predict what any system will produce, look at what it selects for — not at what it says it wants.
