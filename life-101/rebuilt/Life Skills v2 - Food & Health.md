Life Skills v2 - Food & Health
    Nutrition Without Nonsense
        Nutrition — the five principles that actually matter? >>>
            - calorie balance
            - protein priority
            - fiber focus
            - micronutrient density
            - hydration habit
        Calorie balance principle :: Calories in vs calories out determines weight change; food quality affects everything else, but not weight itself
        TDEE (total daily energy expenditure): {{BMR x activity factor}} = your maintenance calories
        Calorie deficit for weight loss :: A 500 calorie/day deficit is roughly 1 pound per week of loss
        Food quality — what it actually buys you? >> Satiety, health, steady energy — not weight loss by itself; that is calorie balance
        Daily protein target? >> 0.7-1 g per pound of body weight
        Protein — why it leads the macro list? >> Preserves muscle, has the highest thermic effect, and is the most satiating macronutrient
        Complete protein sources :: Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, soy, quinoa
        Combining incomplete proteins :: Beans + rice or peanut butter + whole grain together cover the full amino-acid profile
            Daily fiber target ;; 25-35 g
            Fiber — what it buys you ;; Satiety, digestive health, cholesterol reduction, blood sugar control
        Best fiber sources :: Beans, vegetables, whole grains, fruits
        Fiber ramp-up rule :: Increase by about 5 g per week — jumping straight to target causes digestive upset
        Micronutrient density strategy :: Eat a variety of colors — each pigment family carries different vitamins and phytonutrients
            Rainbow method — warm colors ;; Red signals lycopene, orange signals beta-carotene
            Rainbow method — green, purple, white ;; Green signals folate, blue/purple anthocyanins, white allicin
        Supplementation reality check :: Most people eating a varied diet need only vitamin D, plus B12 if vegan
        Daily hydration rule of thumb :: Half your body weight in ounces — a folk baseline, not hard science; thirst and urine color are the real gauges
        Hydration timing optimization :: 16 oz on waking, 8 oz before meals, stop 2 hours before bed
            The hydration heuristic — its honest weakness? >> The popular formulas (half your body weight in ounces, eight glasses a day) have no strong evidential basis. ##theory
                A large share of intake comes from food, and thirst regulates well in healthy adults. Contested: treat the numbers as loose prompts, not targets, and pay attention in heat, illness, and hard exercise where thirst lags need.
        Urine color as hydration gauge :: Aim for pale yellow — dark means dehydrated, fully clear means overhydrated
    Food Safety — Temperatures and Times
        Scope note (not a card) — all temperatures in this section are verified against USDA/FSIS guidance as of August 2026. The old 2-2-2 rule is retired below; do not resurrect it.
        The Danger Zone (food safety)? >> 40-140°F, where bacteria can double every 20 minutes ##safety
            The Danger Zone — why 40 and 140 specifically? >> Below about 40°F bacterial reproduction slows to near-stalled; above about 140°F proteins denature faster than the bacteria can divide. ##safety
                Between them, conditions are near-optimal — which is why the rule is about time spent in the band, not about whether food looks or smells wrong.
        Room temperature limit for perishable food? >> 2 hours maximum — only 1 hour if the ambient temperature is above 90°F ##safety
        Safe Internal Temperatures
            Ground beef, pork, or lamb — safe internal temperature? >> 160°F (USDA) ##safety
            Ground poultry (chicken or turkey) — safe internal temperature? >> 165°F (USDA) — ground poultry is NOT covered by the ground-meat number ##safety
                Why ground poultry needs 165°F when other ground meat needs 160? >> The target is set by the specific pathogens each meat commonly carries and the temperature at which they are reliably killed — poultry's Salmonella and Campylobacter require the higher number. ##safety
                    Same grinding problem, different organisms, different threshold.
            Ground meats — the v1 card's unsafe error? >> The old card taught one temperature for all ground meat; ground beef/pork/lamb need 160°F but ground poultry needs 165°F — two separate numbers, and the lower one undercooks poultry ##safety
            Ground meat — why it needs a higher temperature than whole cuts? >> Grinding mixes surface bacteria throughout the interior, so the very center must reach a kill temperature ##safety
            Whole poultry — safe internal temperature? >> 165°F, including the stuffing if present ##safety
            Whole cuts of beef, pork, or lamb — safe internal temperature? >> 145°F with a 3-minute rest before cutting ##safety
            Fish — safe internal temperature? >> 145°F, or until opaque and flaking ##safety
            Egg dishes — safe internal temperature? >> 160°F, or cook until both white and yolk are firm ##safety
            Reheating leftovers — target temperature? >> 165°F throughout, every time ##safety
            Food thermometer placement :: The thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat, and gristle ##safety
        Cooling and Leftovers (replaces the old 2-2-2 rule)
            The 2-2-2 rule — status? 1. >>> ##safety
                1. room temp max 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F)
                2. two-stage cooling (140 to 70°F within 2 hours, then 70 to 40°F within 4 more)
                3. refrigerated cooked leftovers keep 3-4 days
                4. reheat to 165°F
            Two-stage cooling — stage 1? >> Cool cooked food from 140°F down to 70°F within 2 hours ##safety
                Two-stage cooling — why the curve has two breakpoints? >> The first stage is urgent because 140 to 70°F crosses the fastest-growth band, so it gets the tight two-hour limit. ##safety
                    Below 70°F growth has already slowed considerably, so the second stage can take four hours. The curve matches the bacterial growth rate rather than dividing the time evenly.
            Two-stage cooling — stage 2? >> Continue from 70°F down to 40°F within 4 more hours — 6 hours maximum start to finish ##safety
            Cooling speed tricks :: Shallow containers, small portions, an ice bath, stirring — and yes, hot food can go straight into the fridge in shallow containers ##safety
                Cooked leftovers — refrigerator life ;; 3-4 days; freeze anything you will not eat by then ##safety
        Refrigerator Storage Limits (40°F or below)
            Raw ground meat — fridge life? >> 1-2 days ##safety
            Raw poultry — fridge life? >> 1-2 days ##safety
            Raw steaks or chops — fridge life? >> 3-5 days ##safety
            Soups and stews — fridge life? >> 3-4 days ##safety
            Opened deli meat — fridge life? >> 3-5 days ##safety
        Freezer Storage Limits (0°F or below)
            Ground meat — freezer quality window? >> 3-4 months
            Steaks and chops — freezer quality window? >> 4-12 months
            Whole poultry — freezer quality window? >> 12 months
            Cooked meals — freezer quality window? >> 2-3 months
            Freezer time limits — what they actually measure? >> Quality, not safety — food held at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, it just degrades in taste and texture
    Food Safety — Cross-Contamination and Thawing
        Cross-contamination :: Bacteria from raw meat, poultry, seafood, or egg juices reaching food that will not be cooked again — produce, cooked food, ready-to-eat items ##safety
        The two-cutting-board rule :: One board for raw meat, poultry, and seafood; a separate board for produce and ready-to-eat food ##safety
        Raw meat placement in the refrigerator? >> Bottom shelf, on a plate or in a bag, so juices cannot drip onto food below ##safety
        What is the answer on you rinse raw chicken before cooking? >> No — — washing splashes bacteria onto the sink and counters; cooking to 165°F is what kills it (USDA) ##safety
        Kitchen handwashing rule :: 20 seconds with soap — before cooking, and again after any contact with raw meat or eggs ##safety
        Kitchen work order against cross-contamination :: Prep produce first and raw meat last — or wash the board, knife, and your hands with hot soapy water in between ##safety
        The plate rule (grilling) :: Never return cooked meat to the unwashed plate that carried it raw ##safety
        Used marinade rule :: Never reuse marinade that touched raw meat unless you boil it first ##safety
        The three safe thawing methods? >>> ##safety
            - refrigerator
            - cold water
            - microwave — never the counter
        Refrigerator thawing :: Slowest but safest — the food stays safe the entire time and can be refrozen without cooking
        Cold-water thawing :: Sealed bag, fully submerged, change the water every 30 minutes; cook before refreezing ##safety
        Microwave thawing :: Fastest, but edges start to cook — so cook immediately afterward ##safety
        Counter thawing — why it is never safe? >> The outer layer sits in the Danger Zone for hours while the center is still frozen ##safety
    Foodborne Illness Response
        Food poisoning — first response? >> Fluids in small, frequent sips — dehydration is the main danger of ordinary cases, and most resolve on their own within a day or two ##safety
        Food poisoning — when to seek medical care (CDC)? >>> ##safety
            - bloody diarrhea
            - fever over 102°F
            - diarrhea lasting more than 3 days
            - vomiting that prevents keeping liquids down
            - signs of dehydration
        Dehydration warning signs :: Little or no urination, dry mouth and throat, dizziness on standing ##safety
        Food poisoning — high-risk groups? >> Pregnant women, adults 65+, children under 5, immunocompromised people should see a doctor sooner, at milder symptoms ##safety
    Basic Cooking Technique
        Seasoning in layers :: Salt at each stage of cooking, tasting as you go — one dump at the end sits on the surface instead of building into the food
        Salting meat — the timing rule? >> Either right before cooking or 40+ minutes ahead (a dry brine); in the window between, salt has pulled moisture out but had no time to reabsorb
        Pasta water salting :: Salt it generously (roughly 1 tablespoon per 4 quarts) — boiling is the only chance to season the noodle itself
        Searing — what it is actually for? >> High heat drives browning (the Maillard reaction) and its flavor compounds — "sealing in juices" is a myth
        Searing prerequisites :: A dry surface and a fully preheated pan — surface moisture must boil off before browning can begin, so wet meat steams gray
        Crowding the pan — named failure mode? >> Too much food at once drops the pan temperature and everything steams instead of sears; cook in batches
        Sweating (technique) :: Cooking aromatics low and slow without browning — onions turn translucent and sweet, never brown
        Heat control — the two default modes? >> Sear hot for browning and crust; sweat low for softening aromatics — most home-cooking errors are running the wrong mode
        Fond :: The browned bits stuck to the pan after searing — concentrated flavor and the base of a pan sauce, not a mess to scrub away
        Deglazing :: Adding liquid to a hot pan and scraping up the fond so it dissolves into the sauce
        Pan sauce — the sequence? 1. >>>
            1. sear the protein and remove it
            2. sauté aromatics in the remaining fat
            3. deglaze with wine or stock
            4. reduce
            5. finish with cold butter off the heat
        Pan sauce — why that order? >> The fond must form before any liquid arrives, and butter breaks if boiled — so browning first, reducing next, enriching last
        Roasting — high-heat range? >> 400-450°F — vegetables and anything that should brown and crisp outside
        Roasting — low-and-slow range? >> 250-325°F — large roasts, where gentle heat cooks the center evenly before the outside overshoots
        Braising — the principle? >> Tough, collagen-rich cuts + low heat + hours + partial liquid — the formula that turns the cheapest cuts into the richest dishes
        Braising — why it works? >> Long, low, moist heat melts collagen into gelatin; the same cut grilled fast would be shoe leather
        Cuts built for braising :: Chuck, short ribs, shanks, pork shoulder — the hard-working muscles
        The pasta-water trick :: Reserve a cup before draining — the starchy, salty water emulsifies the sauce and binds it to the noodles
        Resting meat? >> 5-10 minutes for steaks, 15-30 minutes for roasts — juices redistribute and stay in the meat instead of on the cutting board
        Carryover cooking? >> Internal temperature keeps rising 5-10°F after the meat leaves the heat — pull it early and let the rest finish the job
        The taste-and-adjust loop :: Taste, then ask four questions — needs salt? needs acid? needs fat? needs heat? Fix one thing, taste again
        The acid diagnosis :: A dish that tastes flat but already salty almost always needs acid — lemon or vinegar, not more salt

    Grocery Shopping Optimization
        USDA food plans — the four tiers? >> Thrifty, low-cost, moderate, liberal — the government's reference grocery budgets, updated monthly
        USDA food plan anchor (per adult, monthly)? >> Roughly $300-360 on the thrifty plan up to $490-600 on the liberal plan (as of 2026) ##volatile
        Grocery budget swing factors? >> Location (HCOL +30-50%), dietary restrictions, food waste, and how often you eat out
        The 50/30/20 grocery split :: 50% essentials, 30% variety and preference, 20% treats and convenience
        Store layout psychology :: Essentials are spread around the perimeter so you walk past the impulse-buy center aisles to reach them
        Defensive shopping pattern :: Shop the perimeter first (produce, meat, dairy), then enter specific aisles with a list
        Unit price rule :: Always compare price per ounce or pound, never the package price
        Store brand economics :: Typically 25% cheaper with the same quality for basics — often from the same factory
        Grocery sale cycles? >> Most items rotate on a 6-8 week cycle — buy a 12-week supply at the low
        Bulk buying — the three-condition test? >>>
            - you will use it before expiration
            - you save more than 20%
            - you have the storage
        Best bulk buys :: Non-perishables, frozen foods, paper products, cleaning supplies
        Worst bulk buys :: Produce, bread, condiments, spices — spices lose potency before you finish them
        Loss leader strategy :: Stores deliberately lose money on milk, eggs, bananas to pull you in past everything else
        The eye-level premium? >> Products at eye level cost 15-20% more than the same category on the top or bottom shelf
        The end-cap illusion :: End-of-aisle displays are not necessarily sales — check the unit price before believing the sign
        Checkout temptation pricing :: Items at the register carry roughly 300% markup
        The multi-buy trap? >> "2 for $5" usually means $2.50 each anyway — the framing sells volume, not a discount

            - prep (2-3 hours)

        Cross-utilization (meal planning) :: Pick recipes that share ingredients so nothing gets bought for a single use

    Health Insurance — Plan Types and Vocabulary
        Scope note (not a card) — this and all following sections are US-specific. This module merges the two prior health-insurance treatments (the "Deep Dive" that had been misfiled under Tax Planning, and the Healthcare tree's version) into one canon; neither survives separately.
        HMO (Health Maintenance Organization) :: Primary care physician required, referrals needed for specialists, lowest premiums, no out-of-network coverage ##us
        PPO (Preferred Provider Organization) :: No PCP or referrals required, higher premiums, out-of-network coverage available ##us
        EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization) :: No PCP or referrals required, but no out-of-network coverage except emergencies — the middle-ground option ##us
        HDHP (High Deductible Health Plan) :: Lower premiums, higher deductible, and it is the plan type that unlocks HSA eligibility ##us
        Premium (insurance) :: The monthly payment you make whether or not you use any care
        Deductible (insurance) :: What you pay out of pocket before insurance starts covering care
            Copayment ;; A fixed dollar amount per service — e.g., $30 for a doctor visit
        Coinsurance :: The percentage of costs you pay after meeting the deductible — e.g., 20%
        Out-of-pocket maximum :: The most you can pay for covered care in a year; after hitting it, insurance pays 100% of covered services
        Health plan true cost :: Annual premiums + deductible + expected copays, sanity-checked against the out-of-pocket maximum as the worst case
    Health Insurance — Choosing a Plan
        High-utilizer plan rule? >> Expecting over $5,000 in care (chronic conditions, planned procedures) — take the low deductible, even at a higher premium ##us
            The $5,000 utilization threshold — why it flips there? >> Below the crossover, the premium savings of a high-deductible plan exceed the extra out-of-pocket you will actually incur. ##us
                Above it, you will hit the deductible regardless, so the higher premium buys real coverage instead of insurance you never reach. Re-derive it from your own plan's numbers rather than memorizing 5,000.
        Low-utilizer plan rule? >> Expecting under $1,000 in care (young and healthy) — the HDHP + HSA combination usually wins ##us
        HDHP + HSA — why the combo wins for the healthy? >> Premium savings plus the HSA's triple tax advantage compound during the years you barely use care ##us
        Plan break-even analysis :: Compare the annual premium difference between two plans to the deductible difference — the premium gap buys you a smaller deductible or it doesn't
            The plan break-even — why it is just two subtractions? >> A premium difference is certain and a deductible difference is contingent, so the comparison is whether the guaranteed extra premium buys more than the contingent extra exposure.
                Everything else in plan selection is noise until those two numbers are on the table.
        Pre-enrollment network check :: Verify your specific doctors are in-network before enrolling — directories go stale, so call the office and ask
    Health Insurance — Networks, Authorizations, and Appeals
        Out-of-network cost multiple? >> 2-10x more, often with a separate, higher deductible ##us
        Balance billing :: An out-of-network provider billing you the difference between their charge and the insurer's allowed amount ##us
        No Surprises Act protection :: For emergencies and for out-of-network providers at in-network facilities, you owe only in-network cost sharing — ground ambulances are the big exception (as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        Continuity of care (network exit) :: If your doctor leaves the network mid-treatment, you can sometimes continue at in-network rates temporarily — ask the insurer directly ##us
        Prior authorization — what usually requires it? >> Expensive drugs, specialist procedures, imaging, medical equipment ##us
        Prior authorization timeframes? >> Urgent decisions in about 72 hours; standard commonly 7-14 days, with federal rules now pushing government-program plans to 7 (as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        Claim denial appeal ladder? 1. >>> ##us
            1. internal appeal with the insurer
            2. external review by an independent reviewer
            3. state insurance commissioner
        Internal appeal success rate? >> Roughly 40-44% of appealed denials get overturned (ACA marketplace data, as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        External review success rate? >> Independent reviewers overturn roughly 40-50% of the denials that reach them (as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        The appeal asymmetry :: Fewer than 1% of denials are ever appealed, yet about 40% of appeals win — the denial letter is priced on the bet that you won't bother ##us
    Getting Care — ER, Urgent Care, or Telehealth
        Go to the ER (call 911) for? >>> ##safety
            - chest pain
            - stroke signs (face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech)
            - severe bleeding
            - trouble breathing
            - sudden severe headache or confusion
            - loss of consciousness — recognition protocols live in Emergency Preparedness & First Aid
        Urgent care is for :: Stitches, sprains, minor burns, simple fractures, infections needing same-day attention — problems that need treatment today but are not life-threatening ##safety
        Telehealth is for :: Rashes, prescription refills, triage, cold and flu questions, mental-health follow-ups — anything diagnosable through a conversation or a camera ##safety
        Urgent care's hard limits :: No chest pain, stroke signs, or severe breathing trouble — urgent care will just send you on to the ER, and the detour spends the treatment window ##safety
        Urgent care vs ER — the price asymmetry? >> Urgent care $150-300 vs ER $1,500-3,000 for the same non-emergency
        Where-to-go tiebreaker? >> Genuinely unsure whether it's an emergency — call 911 or go to the ER; telehealth and urgent care can escalate you upward, but only when there is time to spare ##safety
    Preventive Care and Screenings
        ACA preventive care rule :: In-network preventive services are covered at 100%, no cost sharing — physicals, screenings, and CDC-recommended vaccines (as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        Preventive coverage — the coding catch? >> The visit must be coded as preventive, not diagnostic — mention a symptom and the same visit can become billable, so ask how it will be coded before and during ##us
            The preventive-coding catch — why a code changes the price? >> Insurers are required to cover certain preventive services at no cost-sharing, and the billing code is what determines whether a visit legally counts as one. ##us
                Mentioning a symptom can convert a screening into a diagnostic visit, which falls under your deductible — the medicine is identical and the code is not.
        No-cost annual screenings :: Blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, depression, STIs at the annual physical ##us
        Covered immunizations :: All CDC-recommended vaccines — flu, COVID, and shingles (50+) among them ##us
        Women's preventive coverage :: Birth control, well-woman visits, breastfeeding support ##us
            Mammogram — current guidance ;; Start at 40, every two years through 74 (USPSTF, as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        Colorectal cancer screening — current start age? >> 45 (USPSTF, as of 2026) — moved down from the 50 the old cards taught ##us ##volatile
        Cervical cancer screening — start age? >> 21 ##us
        Lung cancer screening — who qualifies? >> Ages 50-80 with a 20 pack-year smoking history who still smoke or quit within 15 years (USPSTF, as of 2026) ##us ##volatile
        Screening additions in your 20s :: Annual physical, cholesterol, blood pressure, STI screening, skin check
        Screening additions in your 30s :: Diabetes screening if overweight, baseline EKG
        Screening additions in your 40s :: Mammogram (40), colorectal screening (45), comprehensive eye exam
        Screening additions in your 50s :: Shingles vaccine, hearing test, prostate discussion (men)
        Screening additions in your 60s and beyond? >> AAA screening (men 65-75 who ever smoked), bone density (women 65+), cognitive and fall-risk assessment
    Healthcare Cost Reduction
        Generic drug savings? >> 80-85% cheaper on average, with the identical active ingredient (FDA)
        Generic drug standards :: Generics must meet the same FDA standards for safety, strength, and efficacy as the brand
        Pharmacy price arbitrage :: The same prescription varies up to 10x between pharmacies — call around or use a comparison tool before filling
            Pharmacy price variance — why the same drug costs 10x more across town? >> Cash prices are set independently by each pharmacy and by pharmacy benefit manager contracts that are not public, so there is no single market price and no mechanism forcing convergence. ##us
                Prices vary because nothing makes them agree, which is exactly why comparison works.
        Prescription discount programs :: GoodRx, pharmacy clubs, manufacturer coupons — sometimes beat your insurance price, so ask the counter for both prices
        90-day supply economics :: Often cheaper per dose, and one copay instead of three
            Mail-order pharmacy ;; Usually 20-30% cheaper for maintenance medications
        Therapeutic alternatives :: Ask the prescriber for a cheaper drug in the same class — often near-equivalent at a fraction of the price
        Medical bill audit — the sequence? 1. >>>
            1. request an itemized bill
            2. match it line-by-line against the EOB
            3. verify the CPT codes describe services you actually received
            4. dispute every error in writing
        Medical bill audit — why itemized comes first? >> The summary bill hides duplicate charges, errors, and upcoding — you cannot audit a single-line total
        Medical bill error rate :: Common enough that auditing is positive expected value — billing advocates claim a large majority of bills contain errors; even conservative audits find them routinely
            Medical bill cash discount ;; Offering immediate payment commonly gets 20-50% off
        Medical payment plans :: Most providers offer 0% interest plans — take the plan before ever touching a credit card for a medical bill
        Hospital financial assistance (charity care) :: ACA section 501(r) requires nonprofit hospitals to maintain a written financial assistance policy — ask for it by name before paying anything ##us
        Charity care eligibility? >> Thresholds vary by hospital and state — free care commonly at or below about 200% of the federal poverty level, with sliding-scale discounts to 300-400% ##us
        Old medical debt settlement? >> Offer a 30-50% lump sum — collectors bought the debt for pennies on the dollar

    Aging Parents and Caregiving
        The most expensive misconception in elder care? >> That Medicare pays for long-term care. ##us ##volatile
            It does not. Medicare covers short-term skilled nursing after a qualifying hospital stay — up to 100 days, with cost-sharing after day 20 — and does not cover custodial care, which is help with bathing, dressing, eating, and toileting. That is the care most people actually need, and it is paid privately or by Medicaid.
        Who actually pays for long-term care in the US? >> Private savings first, then long-term care insurance if it exists, then Medicaid once assets are spent down below the state's threshold. ##us ##volatile
            Medicaid is the country's real long-term care program, and it is means-tested by design.
        Rough national median costs of care (2025 survey data)? >> In-home aide and assisted living each land in the mid-five-figures to roughly $70,000-plus a year ##us ##volatile
            memory care runs meaningfully higher; a semi-private nursing home room is around $115,000 a year and a private room around $130,000. Costs vary enormously by state — coastal and urban markets run well above these.
        Why to have the conversation before a crisis? >> After a stroke or a fall, decisions get made in days by whoever is in the room, usually without the documents and without knowing what the person would have wanted.
            Every good option in elder care requires lead time, and a crisis is precisely when there is none.
        How to open the conversation with a parent who does not want to have it? >> Ask about their wishes rather than their decline, and make it reciprocal
            "I'm getting my own documents in order, and I realized I don't know what you'd want." Anchoring it to your own planning removes the implication that you think they are failing.
        The document set to locate while everyone is healthy? >> Advance directive, healthcare proxy or medical power of attorney, financial power of attorney, will and any trust, insurance policies including any long-term care policy, a current medication list, the provider list, and account access. ##us
            Knowing they exist is not enough — you need to know where they physically are.
        Why the financial power of attorney is the one people forget? >> Without it, if a parent becomes unable to manage money, no one can act for them without going to court for a conservatorship ##us
            which is slow, public, expensive, and avoidable with one signed document beforehand.
        The levels of care, from least to most intensive? >>>
            - In-home help (a few hours a week to full-time)
            - adult day programs
            - assisted living (housing plus help with daily activities)
            - memory care (secured, dementia-specific staffing)
            - skilled nursing (medical care around the clock). Most families move up this ladder later than they should
        What a geriatric care manager does, and when one is worth it? >> A licensed professional who assesses needs, navigates local options, and coordinates care — typically paid hourly.
            Worth it when you live far away, when the situation is medically complex, or when siblings disagree, because they convert an argument into an assessment.
        The warning signs that independent living is failing? >> Unopened mail and unpaid bills, expired or spoiled food, weight loss, new dents in the car, missed medications, wearing the same clothes repeatedly, and withdrawal from things they used to do.
            Any two together warrant a real conversation.
        Why caregiver burnout is a safety issue and not a feelings issue? >> An exhausted caregiver makes medication errors, misses warning signs, and eventually has their own health event — at which point there are two people needing care. ##safety
            Respite is maintenance on the caregiver, and it is Pattern 1 applied to a person.
        What respite care actually is? >> Short-term relief — a few hours a week, an adult day program, or a brief facility stay — specifically so the primary caregiver can rest.
            Many people learn it exists only after they are already past the point of needing it.
        The sandwich-generation squeeze, stated plainly :: Supporting aging parents and dependent children at once, usually during peak earning years that are also peak retirement-saving years. ##us
            The failure mode is funding everyone else and quietly stopping your own retirement contributions — which converts one generation's crisis into the next one's.
        The one thing to do this month if all of this feels far off? >> Find out whether your parents have an advance directive, a healthcare proxy, and a financial power of attorney, and where they are kept. ##us
            That single question is most of the value, and it costs one phone call.
